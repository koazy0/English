## 正文

When economists and policymakers discuss environmental threats, climate change dominates the conversation. Yet another crisis, equally serious, receives far less attention: the accelerating loss of biodiversity. The extinction of species and the degradation of ecosystems are occurring at rates not seen since the disappearance of the dinosaurs, and the economic consequences are far more severe than most people realise. Protecting biodiversity is not merely a moral obligation — it is an economic imperative.

The numbers are alarming. According to the World Wildlife Fund, global wildlife populations have declined by an average of 69 percent since 1970. Approximately one million species currently face extinction, many within decades. Tropical forests, which harbour over half of all terrestrial species, are being destroyed at a rate of roughly ten million hectares per year. Coral reefs, which support roughly a quarter of all marine species, have declined by 50 percent since the 1950s. These are not abstract statistics; they represent the dismantling of the natural systems upon which human prosperity depends.

The economic case for conservation begins with food security. Three-quarters of the world's food crops depend to some degree on animal pollination, a service provided largely by insects such as bees, butterflies, and beetles. The annual value of pollination to global agriculture is estimated at 235 to 577 billion dollars. Yet pollinator populations are declining rapidly due to habitat loss, pesticide use, and climate change. Without pollinators, yields of fruits, vegetables, nuts, and coffee would collapse, driving up food prices and threatening nutrition for billions of people.

Medicine provides another powerful argument. Approximately 50 percent of modern pharmaceuticals are derived from or inspired by natural compounds. Aspirin came from willow bark, the cancer drug paclitaxel from the Pacific yew tree, and the antimalarial artemisinin from sweet wormwood. Each species that goes extinct represents a potential medical breakthrough that will never be discovered. The economic value of nature-based drug discovery, while difficult to quantify precisely, runs into hundreds of billions of dollars.

Beyond these direct benefits, ecosystems provide services whose value is enormous but often invisible. Wetlands filter water and reduce flood risk — services that would cost trillions to replicate with engineered infrastructure. Forests absorb carbon dioxide and regulate regional rainfall patterns. Mangroves protect coastlines from storm surges, saving communities from billions in damage. When these ecosystems are destroyed, the costs do not disappear; they are simply deferred, to be paid later in the form of disaster recovery, water treatment, and emergency response.

Despite this overwhelming economic case, investment in biodiversity conservation remains grossly inadequate. Global spending on biodiversity is estimated at 78 to 91 billion dollars per year — a fraction of the 500 to 700 billion dollars that experts say is needed. Meanwhile, governments continue to subsidise activities that harm biodiversity — including fossil fuel extraction, intensive agriculture, and overfishing — to the tune of roughly 500 billion dollars annually. The perverse incentive structure is clear: we are paying to destroy the natural capital upon which our economy depends.

Changing course will require a fundamental shift in how societies value nature. This means integrating biodiversity into economic decision-making, eliminating harmful subsidies, and creating financial mechanisms — such as green bonds and biodiversity credits — that channel investment toward conservation. The economic logic is straightforward: protecting nature costs far less than replacing the services it provides. The question is whether humanity will recognise this truth before the losses become irreversible.

## 长难句解析

**1.** Each species that goes extinct represents a potential medical breakthrough that will never be discovered.

- **拆分**：两个 that 分别引导定语从句，第一个修饰 species，第二个修饰 breakthrough。
- **翻译**：每一个灭绝的物种都代表着一个永远无法被发现的潜在医学突破。

**2.** Meanwhile, governments continue to subsidise activities that harm biodiversity — including fossil fuel extraction, intensive agriculture, and overfishing — to the tune of roughly 500 billion dollars annually.

- **拆分**：that 引导定语从句修饰 activities，破折号之间为举例说明，to the tune of 为固定搭配，意为"达到……的数额"。
- **翻译**：与此同时，各国政府继续补贴损害生物多样性的活动——包括化石燃料开采、集约化农业和过度捕捞——每年总额约5000亿美元。

## 文章大意

本文论述了生物多样性丧失的经济后果。全球野生动物种群自1970年以来平均下降69%，约100万种面临灭绝。经济论据首先涉及粮食安全——全球75%的粮食作物依赖动物授粉，授粉服务价值达2350至5770亿美元。其次是医药——约50%的现代药物源自天然化合物，每个灭绝物种都可能意味着一个医学突破的丧失。此外，生态系统提供的无形服务（湿地净水、森林固碳、红树林防风暴）价值数万亿。然而保护投资严重不足，而有害补贴却高达5000亿美元。文章呼吁将生物多样性纳入经济决策，消除有害补贴，建立绿色金融机制。保护自然的成本远低于替代其服务的成本。

## 思考题

**1.** According to the passage, the economic argument for protecting biodiversity includes all of the following EXCEPT:

A. the value of pollination for global agriculture
B. the potential for discovering new medicines from natural compounds
C. the revenue generated by eco-tourism
D. the ecosystem services that would cost trillions to replace artificially

**2.** The phrase "perverse incentive structure" (Paragraph 6) refers to the fact that:

A. conservation programmes are too expensive to maintain
B. governments pay more to destroy biodiversity than to protect it
C. pharmaceutical companies refuse to invest in nature-based research
D. farmers cannot afford to stop using pesticides

**3.** The author's main argument is that:

A. climate change is a more serious threat than biodiversity loss
B. biodiversity conservation is primarily a moral rather than economic concern
C. protecting biodiversity makes economic sense, but current incentives work against it
D. technology can fully replace the services that ecosystems provide

2. **B** — "不合理的激励结构"指政府每年补贴破坏生物多样性的活动约5000亿美元，而保护投资仅780至910亿美元，花钱破坏而非保护。
