# The Largest Undocumented Disparity: Gestational Diabetes and Chinese American Women

Dr. Maria Chen first noticed something troubling in her obstetrics practice in San Francisco's Chinatown nearly a decade ago. Her Chinese American patients were developing gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) at rates that seemed far higher than what the textbooks predicted. When she mentioned this to colleagues, the response was familiar: Asian Americans are at higher risk for type 2 diabetes, so naturally their gestational diabetes rates would be higher too. But when Chen looked at the data more carefully, she found something the medical establishment had overlooked. The spike in GDM among Chinese American women was outpacing even the rise in type 2 diabetes, and it was doing so at a speed that could not be explained by genetic predisposition alone.

Gestational diabetes, which develops during pregnancy and typically resolves after delivery, affects approximately 6 to 9 percent of pregnancies in the general American population. Among Chinese American women, recent studies suggest the rate may be as high as 15 to 20 percent — more than double the national average. Yet this disparity has received remarkably little attention from researchers or public health authorities. One epidemiologist called it "the largest undocumented disparity in maternal health."

Part of the reason it has gone unnoticed is the way medical data is collected. Asian Americans are frequently aggregated into a single category in health studies, obscuring enormous variation between subgroups. Chinese, Indian, Filipino, and Vietnamese Americans have vastly different health profiles, but when their data is merged, important patterns vanish. Even when Chinese Americans are studied separately, the sample sizes are often too small to draw statistically significant conclusions.

The more insidious problem is that the standard screening tools may be failing the very population most at risk. Body mass index (BMI) thresholds for GDM screening were developed based on predominantly white populations and do not account for the fact that Asian bodies carry fat differently. A Chinese American woman with a BMI of 23 — considered normal by World Health Organization standards — may have a body fat percentage equivalent to a white woman with a BMI of 28, who would be classified as overweight and automatically screened. Under current guidelines, the Chinese American woman may never be flagged as at-risk.

This mismatch has concrete consequences. Undiagnosed GDM increases the risk of preeclampsia, premature delivery, and stillbirth. Children born to mothers with uncontrolled GDM are more likely to develop obesity and type 2 diabetes later in life, creating an intergenerational cycle of metabolic disease. The American Diabetes Association has acknowledged the screening gap but has been slow to revise its guidelines, citing insufficient evidence — a circular problem, since the lack of subgroup-specific data makes it difficult to generate the evidence needed for revision.

Chen and a growing number of researchers are now calling for ethnicity-specific BMI thresholds, larger studies of Asian American subgroups, and universal GDM screening regardless of BMI. The cost of inaction, they argue, is measured not in statistical abstractions but in real pregnancies, real complications, and real families.
