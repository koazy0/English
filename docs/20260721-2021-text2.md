## 正文

The food delivery industry has exploded in recent years. Platforms like Uber Eats, DoorDash, and Deliveroo now operate in hundreds of cities worldwide, promising consumers hot meals at the tap of a button. The convenience is undeniable. But behind the seamless app interface lies a growing controversy over the rights and welfare of the workers who make it all possible.

At the heart of the debate is the employment status of delivery riders. Most are classified as independent contractors rather than employees. This classification saves platforms billions in payroll taxes, health insurance, and pension contributions. For the workers, it means no guaranteed minimum wage, no paid sick leave, and no protection against unfair dismissal. A rider who falls ill or is injured on the job receives nothing from the platform that depends on their labour.

The numbers tell a troubling story. Surveys of gig economy workers across Europe and North America consistently report that a majority earn less than the local minimum wage once vehicle costs, insurance, and waiting time are factored in. In the United Kingdom, a government review found that many riders earn as little as 3 pounds per hour during quiet periods. Turnover is extraordinarily high, with most workers leaving within six months.

Platforms defend their model by pointing to flexibility. Riders, they argue, choose when and how much to work, enjoying a freedom that traditional employment cannot offer. There is some truth in this: the ability to log on and off at will suits students, caregivers, and others who need to fit work around other commitments. Yet critics contend that this flexibility is largely illusory. Algorithmic management systems penalise riders who decline jobs or go offline during peak hours, reducing their access to lucrative assignments. What appears to be freedom is in practice a sophisticated form of control.

The legal landscape is shifting. In 2021, the UK Supreme Court ruled that Uber drivers are workers, not contractors, entitling them to minimum wage and holiday pay. Similar rulings have followed in Spain, the Netherlands, and parts of the United States. California's Proposition 22, which exempted gig companies from providing full employee benefits, was struck down by a state court in 2021, though the legal battle continues.

These rulings reflect a growing consensus that the current model is unsustainable. Yet reform carries risks. Platforms warn that reclassifying riders as employees would force them to raise prices, reduce availability, and cut jobs — potentially harming the very workers reform is designed to help. Whether this is a genuine warning or a negotiating tactic remains a matter of heated dispute.

What is clear is that the food delivery economy, for all its convenience, rests on a foundation of precarious labour. Resolving the tension between flexibility and security will be one of the defining labour challenges of the decade. Consumers who enjoy their meals delivered in minutes may need to consider what — and whom — they are truly paying for.

## 文章大意

本文讨论外卖行业零工劳动者的权益问题。文章首先描述外卖行业的繁荣与争议核心——骑手被归类为独立承包商而非雇员，因此缺乏基本劳动保障；然后用数据揭示多数骑手实际收入低于最低工资；接着分析平台所谓"灵活性"的虚假性——算法系统实际上在控制骑手；再介绍各国法律层面的变化趋势；最后指出改革虽有必要但存在风险，消费者也需要反思便利背后的劳动代价。
