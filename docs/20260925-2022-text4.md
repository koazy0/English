## 正文

Cities are hot — significantly hotter than the surrounding countryside. This phenomenon, known as the urban heat island effect, occurs because concrete, asphalt, and steel absorb and retain heat far more effectively than vegetation and soil. On a summer afternoon, a city centre can be 5 to 10 degrees Celsius warmer than nearby rural areas. As climate change pushes temperatures higher and urbanisation continues apace, the problem is becoming more dangerous — and green roofs are emerging as one of the most promising solutions.

A green roof is exactly what it sounds like: a rooftop covered with living plants growing in a specially engineered growing medium. They range from extensive systems with shallow soil and hardy sedums to intensive gardens that support shrubs, trees, and even vegetable plots. What they all share is the capacity to cool buildings and their surroundings through evapotranspiration — the process by which plants release water vapour, absorbing heat in the process. Studies have shown that green roofs can reduce rooftop surface temperatures by 30 to 40 degrees Celsius compared with conventional roofs, and lower the ambient air temperature above them by 2 to 5 degrees.

The benefits extend well beyond cooling. Green roofs absorb rainwater, reducing the volume of stormwater runoff that overwhelms urban drainage systems during heavy downpours. A typical extensive green roof retains 50 to 70 percent of annual rainfall, easing the burden on sewers and reducing the risk of flooding. They also provide habitat for pollinators and birds in environments where green space is scarce. In cities like London and Toronto, green roofs have become stepping stones in ecological corridors that support urban biodiversity. Moreover, they insulate buildings, cutting energy consumption for both heating and cooling by 15 to 25 percent, and they extend the lifespan of the underlying roof membrane by shielding it from ultraviolet radiation and temperature fluctuations.

Despite these advantages, adoption remains slow. Cost is the primary barrier. Installing a green roof typically costs two to three times more than a conventional roof, and maintenance requirements add to the ongoing expense. Building codes in most cities do not require green roofs, and the financial benefits — lower energy bills, reduced stormwater fees — accrue gradually over decades rather than immediately. For developers building to sell, the economics often do not align: they bear the upfront cost, but the long-term savings go to future owners.

Some cities have found ways to overcome these obstacles. In 2009, Toronto became the first major city to mandate green roofs on new commercial and residential buildings above a certain size. Copenhagen followed in 2010, requiring green roofs on all new buildings with roof slopes below 30 degrees. In the United States, cities like Denver, San Francisco, and New York have adopted combinations of requirements and incentives, including tax credits, density bonuses, and grants. These pioneering programmes demonstrate that with the right policy framework, green roofs can move from novelty to norm.

The green roof movement is ultimately about reimagining the city itself — not as a wasteland of concrete, but as a living landscape. As the climate crisis intensifies, the roofs above our heads may become as important as the parks at our feet.
