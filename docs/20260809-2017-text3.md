## 正文

For more than half a century, Gross Domestic Product has been the single most important measure of a nation's success. When GDP rises, politicians claim credit; when it falls, alarm bells ring. Governments around the world shape their policies around the goal of maximising GDP growth. Yet a growing chorus of economists and policymakers argue that this obsession with a single number is not merely misleading but potentially harmful.

The limitations of GDP as a measure of well-being are well documented. GDP counts the money spent on rebuilding after a natural disaster as positive growth, even though the community is plainly worse off. It includes the revenue generated by cigarette sales but ignores the healthcare costs of smoking-related diseases. It rises when people work longer hours but says nothing about whether those people are happy, healthy, or fulfilled. In short, GDP measures the size of economic activity but says nothing about its quality or distribution.

The gap between GDP and well-being is not theoretical — it is visible in the data. The United States has the largest economy in the world, yet it ranks well below many smaller nations in measures of life expectancy, infant mortality, and social mobility. China's GDP has grown spectacularly over four decades, lifting hundreds of millions from poverty, yet its cities suffer from severe air pollution, and surveys show declining levels of life satisfaction. India's economic growth has been impressive, but malnutrition rates among children remain among the highest globally.

Several alternative indicators have been proposed. The United Nations' Human Development Index combines GDP with measures of education and life expectancy. The tiny kingdom of Bhutan famously adopted "Gross National Happiness" as its guiding metric, surveying citizens on their psychological well-being, health, and community vitality. New Zealand has introduced a "well-being budget" that allocates spending based on broader measures of social health. The OECD's Better Life Index allows citizens to compare countries across dimensions that matter to them personally.

Yet these alternatives face serious challenges of their own. Happiness is subjective and difficult to measure consistently across cultures. Composite indices require judgements about the relative importance of different factors — judgements that are inevitably political. GDP, for all its flaws, has the advantage of being a single, widely understood number that can be compared across countries and over time.

This does not mean we should abandon GDP, but rather that we should stop treating it as the only measure that matters. No single number can capture the complexity of a nation's well-being. A dashboard of indicators — covering health, education, inequality, environmental quality, and subjective well-being — would provide a far richer picture. The real obstacle is not technical but political: leaders prefer simple stories, and "GDP is up" is a simpler story than "life expectancy has risen but inequality has widened and air quality has deteriorated."

What we measure shapes what we value. If we value only economic growth, we will pursue policies that maximise growth — even at the expense of health, equality, and the environment. Measuring what truly matters is the first step towards pursuing it.

## 长难句解析

**1.** "GDP counts the money spent on rebuilding after a natural disaster as positive growth, even though the community is plainly worse off."

- **结构**: 主句GDP counts...as positive growth（把……算作正向增长）；spent on rebuilding为过去分词短语修饰money；even though引导让步状语从句；worse off为固定搭配，意为"境况更差"
- **翻译**: GDP把自然灾害后重建所花费的钱算作正向增长，尽管社区明显处境更差了

**2.** "The real obstacle is not technical but political: leaders prefer simple stories, and 'GDP is up' is a simpler story than 'life expectancy has risen but inequality has widened and air quality has deteriorated.'"

- **结构**: not...but...连接表语；冒号后解释政治障碍的具体含义；and连接两个并列分句，第二个分句含比较级结构simpler...than...，than后为比较对象——包含三个并列分句的复杂陈述
- **翻译**: 真正的障碍不是技术性的而是政治性的：领导人偏好简单的故事，而"GDP上升了"比"预期寿命提高了但不平等扩大了且空气质量恶化了"是更简单的故事

## 文章大意

本文批判GDP作为衡量国家成功唯一指标的局限性。文章先指出对GDP的执迷可能有害；然后详述GDP的缺陷——它衡量经济活动规模而非质量或分配，将灾害重建计为增长却忽视健康代价；再用美、中、印的实例说明GDP与福祉的差距；接着介绍替代指标（人类发展指数、国民幸福总值、幸福预算等）及其面临的测量和比较难题；最后指出不需要废除GDP而是建立多指标体系，但政治偏好简单叙事构成真正障碍，我们衡量的东西决定了我们追求什么。

## 思考题

**1.** Why does the author mention natural disasters and cigarette sales in the second paragraph?

A. To show that GDP accurately reflects social costs
B. To illustrate that GDP can increase even when well-being decreases
C. To argue that natural disasters are beneficial for economic growth
D. To compare the economic impact of disasters and smoking

<details><summary>答案</summary>B。作者用灾后重建和香烟销售的例子说明GDP衡量经济活动规模但不反映福利质量——灾害重建推高GDP但社区境况更差，这是GDP的"局限性"。</details>

**2.** What is a major challenge facing alternative indicators like Gross National Happiness?

A. They are too expensive for most countries to implement

<details><summary>答案</summary>B。文章第五段指出这些替代指标面临"Happiness is subjective and difficult to measure consistently across cultures"的挑战。</details>

**3.** What does the author mean by "What we measure shapes what we value"?

A. Statistical methods determine political ideology
B. The metrics we choose to track influence the priorities we pursue
C. Value and measurement are completely independent concepts
D. Only economists can decide what a society should value

<details><summary>答案</summary>B。文章末句阐述核心观点：如果我们只重视经济增长，就会追求增长最大化政策——即便以健康、平等和环境为代价；衡量标准决定了追求方向。</details>
