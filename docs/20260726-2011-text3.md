## 正文

Humanity has always sought to build upwards. From the pyramids of Egypt to the medieval cathedrals of Europe, tall structures have served as symbols of power, ambition, and divine aspiration. In the modern era, the skyscraper has become the ultimate expression of this impulse — and the debate over whether taller always means better has never been more relevant.

The race to build higher has accelerated dramatically in the 21st century. In 2000, the world's tallest building was the Petronas Towers in Kuala Lumpur at 452 metres. Today, the Burj Khalifa in Dubai stands at 828 metres, and the Jeddah Tower in Saudi Arabia aims to exceed 1,000 metres when completed. China alone has constructed more skyscrapers in the past fifteen years than the United States built in the entire 20th century. This boom has been driven by a combination of factors: rapid urbanisation, abundant capital, and the desire of emerging economies to announce their arrival on the global stage.

There are sound economic reasons for building tall. In dense cities where land is scarce and expensive, building upwards maximises the value of each square metre of land. Skyscrapers concentrate workers and businesses in compact spaces, reducing commuting times and increasing productivity. Mixed-use towers that combine offices, apartments, shops, and restaurants create vibrant communities where people can live, work, and socialise without ever leaving the building.

Yet the benefits are not as clear-cut as developers claim. Construction costs for super-tall buildings are enormous, and much of the interior space is consumed by elevators, structural supports, and mechanical systems rather than usable floor area. Maintenance costs are similarly staggering: keeping glass facades clean, air-conditioning running, and safety systems operational requires vast ongoing expenditure. Studies have shown that buildings above 300 metres often deliver lower returns per square metre than shorter alternatives.

There are also social and environmental concerns. Tall buildings can cast long shadows that deprive neighbouring areas of sunlight. Wind patterns around skyscrapers can create uncomfortable and even dangerous conditions at street level. Critics argue that the race for height is driven more by ego than by necessity, resulting in buildings that are impressive from a distance but dysfunctional up close.

The skyscraper, then, is a mixed blessing. It represents extraordinary engineering achievement and can make efficient use of scarce urban land. But when the pursuit of height becomes an end in itself rather than a means to better urban living, the costs — economic, social, and environmental — may outweigh the benefits. The real challenge for cities is not to build the tallest building, but to build the best one.

## 长难句解析

**1.** "China alone has constructed more skyscrapers in the past fifteen years than the United States built in the entire 20th century."

- **结构**: 比较级结构more...than...，主句主语China alone，谓语has constructed，宾语more skyscrapers；than引导比较状语从句，从句主语the United States，谓语built，省略了宾语skyscrapers
- **翻译**: 仅中国一国在过去十五年间建造的摩天大楼就超过了美国在整个20世纪建造的总数

**2.** "But when the pursuit of height becomes an end in itself rather than a means to better urban living, the costs — economic, social, and environmental — may outweigh the benefits."

- **结构**: when引导时间/条件状语从句；an end in itself为固定搭配，意为"本身就是目的"；rather than连接对比概念；破折号间为形容词补充说明costs；主句中outweigh意为"超过，胜过"
- **翻译**: 但当追求高度本身成为目的而非改善城市生活的手段时，其经济、社会和环境成本可能超过收益

## 文章大意

本文探讨摩天大楼建设的利弊。文章先回顾人类建造高耸建筑的历史冲动，描述21世纪摩天大楼建设的加速趋势及其驱动因素；然后分析建造高层的经济合理性（节约土地、减少通勤、混合用途）；接着指出超高层建筑的实际问题——建筑和维护成本高昂、可用面积比例低、遮光和风环境问题；最后指出摩天大楼是喜忧参半的存在，追求高度不应成为目的本身，城市建设的真正挑战是建造最好的建筑而非最高的。
