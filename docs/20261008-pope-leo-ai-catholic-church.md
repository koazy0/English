# Pope Leo's Encyclical: Championing Human Imperfection Against the Transhumanist Dream

The most subversive claim in Pope Leo XIV's encyclical on artificial intelligence is not that AI poses risks, or that it needs regulation, or that its benefits should be shared more broadly. These are arguments that anyone who has followed the AI ethics debate has heard a hundred times. The encyclical's truly radical proposition is something far more unsettling to the Silicon Valley worldview: that human imperfection and finitude are not defects to be corrected but the very source of human dignity, beauty, and moral worth — and that AI's attempt to replace human judgment is therefore not merely imprudent but fundamentally immoral.

This argument cuts against the deepest current of technological optimism. The transhumanist movement, which has moved from the margins to the mainstream of Silicon Valley thought, holds that human limitations — our finite lifespan, our cognitive constraints, our emotional vulnerabilities — are problems awaiting technological solutions. Ray Kurzweil's vision of the Singularity, in which human consciousness merges with artificial intelligence and death itself is conquered, is no longer science fiction; it is the implicit framework guiding billions of dollars of investment in life extension, brain-computer interfaces, and artificial general intelligence.

Pope Leo's encyclical does not merely disagree with this vision; it inverts its moral valence. Drawing on the Catholic philosophical tradition, the document argues that finitude is constitutive of meaning. A life without limits is not a liberated life but an empty one, because it is the constraint of time that makes choices meaningful, the constraint of knowledge that makes discovery thrilling, and the constraint of ability that makes effort admirable. Remove the constraints, and you remove the conditions under which human virtues — courage, sacrifice, perseverance, love — become possible.

The encyclical applies this framework to specific AI applications with devastating precision. Autonomous weapons systems that replace the moral agency of soldiers with algorithmic targeting are not merely dangerous; they represent the elimination of moral responsibility from the act of killing. Hiring algorithms that replace human judgment about people with statistical correlations are not merely biased; they represent the abdication of the human obligation to see other people as individuals rather than data points. Surveillance systems that replace human trust with automated monitoring are not merely invasive; they represent the destruction of the social fabric that depends on the possibility of being unobserved.

What makes the encyclical difficult for technologists to dismiss is its refusal to engage on the terrain of practical trade-offs. It does not argue that AI is too risky, or too biased, or too expensive — arguments that invite counterarguments about risk mitigation, bias correction, and cost reduction. It argues that certain applications of AI are wrong in principle, regardless of their practical consequences, because they replace something irreplaceable: the exercise of human judgment in matters that define our humanity.

This is a claim that the AI industry is not prepared to answer, because it cannot be answered with better technology. No improvement in algorithmic fairness addresses the question of whether a machine should make a decision that requires moral reasoning. No enhancement in AI safety resolves the question of whether human vulnerability is a feature or a bug. The encyclical's challenge is not technical but existential, and it will not be resolved by engineering.

## 文章大意

教皇利奥十四世AI通谕最具颠覆性的主张是：人类的不完美和有限性不是需要修正的缺陷，而是尊严、美和道德价值的源泉。这与硅谷的超人类主义愿景根本对立——后者将人类局限性视为待解决的技术问题。通谕将有限性重新定义为意义的构成条件：没有时间约束选择就无意义，没有知识约束发现就无惊喜。通谕将此框架精准应用于自主武器、招聘算法和监控系统，不是批评其实用缺陷，而是质疑其原则性错误——替代了不可替代的人类判断。这一挑战不是技术性的而是存在性的，无法通过工程改进来解决。
