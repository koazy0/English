## 正文

Every profession has its jargon. Doctors have their acronyms, lawyers their Latin phrases, engineers their technical specifications. Specialised language serves a legitimate purpose: it allows experts to communicate precisely and efficiently. But the corporate world has developed a form of jargon that does the opposite — it obscures meaning, inflates importance, and creates distance between those who wield power and those who do not.

Consider a few examples. Companies no longer fire employees; they "rightsize" their workforce. They do not have problems; they face "challenges" that present "opportunities for growth." Managers do not make plans; they develop "strategic roadmaps" and "actionable synergies." Products are not improved; they are "reimagined." On their own, each phrase seems harmless enough. But collectively, they form a language that is designed not to clarify but to conceal.

The pattern is consistent. Unpleasant realities are relabelled in more palatable terms. When a company announces it is "streamlining operations," it usually means people are losing their jobs. When leadership promises to "leverage core competencies," it often signals that they have no concrete plan. The vague, aspirational language fills the vacuum where specifics should be. It sounds impressive without committing to anything measurable.

This linguistic inflation carries real costs. When managers communicate in abstractions, employees struggle to understand what is actually expected of them. Research by the management consultancy Towers Watson found that only 40 percent of employees in large organisations could accurately describe their company's strategy — a failure of communication, not of intelligence. Jargon creates the illusion of alignment while masking genuine disagreement and confusion.

Corporate speak also serves a less innocent function: it reinforces hierarchy. Those who are fluent in the language of "bandwidth," "deliverables," and "stakeholder engagement" signal their membership in the managerial class. Those who are not — frontline workers, new employees, people from different cultural backgrounds — are effectively excluded from conversations that affect their lives. The language becomes a gatekeeping tool, preserving status distinctions that would otherwise need to be justified on their merits.

The irony is that the most effective leaders tend to communicate in plain language. When Howard Schultz returned to Starbucks as CEO during a period of declining sales, he told employees simply: "We are going to fix what needs to be fixed and innovate where we can." No synergies, no roadmaps, no leveraging — just a clear statement of intention. Employees understood exactly what he meant, and the company's performance improved.

None of this is to say that all corporate jargon is evil. Some terms genuinely capture new ideas. But when language becomes a substitute for clear thought rather than a tool for expressing it, something has gone wrong. The words we use shape how we think. If we want organisations that think clearly, we need to start by speaking plainly.

## 文章大意

本文批判企业话术对职场沟通的负面影响。文章先对比专业术语（精确高效）与企业话术（模糊掩盖）的根本区别；然后列举企业话术的典型用例——用美化语言掩盖不愉快的现实；接着分析其三大危害：制造沟通障碍（员工无法理解公司战略）、强化等级制度（话术成为阶层门槛）、替代清晰思维；再用星巴克CEO的正面案例说明简明语言更有效；最后指出语言塑造思维，想要组织清晰思考，需要从说大白话开始。
