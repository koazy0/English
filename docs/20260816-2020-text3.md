## 正文

Every generation believes it speaks differently from the one before it — and for the most part, it is right. Language is not a fixed code but a living system that evolves with the people who use it. Slang, in particular, serves as a linguistic fingerprint of each cohort, encoding attitudes, experiences, and social realities that distinguish one age group from another.

Consider how the vocabulary of young people has shifted over the decades. In the 1960s, the counterculture gave us "groovy" and "far out," words that reflected a rejection of mainstream values. The 1980s brought "yuppie" and "grody," capturing the tension between material ambition and ironic detachment. By the 2000s, text messaging had compressed language into abbreviations like "LOL" and "BRB," driven by the constraints of early mobile phones. Today's young people deploy terms like "rizz," "ghosting," and "vibe check" — words shaped by social media, online gaming, and a heightened awareness of mental health.

Several forces drive this linguistic churn. Technology is the most obvious: each new communication platform creates its own norms and vocabulary. Social media rewards brevity and emotional expressiveness, favouring terms that are punchy and shareable. Culture plays a role too: immigrant communities introduce words from other languages, which then spread into mainstream usage. The English lexicon has absorbed words from Hindi, Yoruba, Japanese, and dozens of other languages, a process that continues today.

But generational language is not merely about vocabulary. It also reflects deeper shifts in worldview. The fact that younger people have coined terms like "doomscrolling" and "burnout" reveals a preoccupation with the psychological effects of constant digital connectivity. The widespread use of "they" as a singular pronoun signals an evolving understanding of gender identity. Words like "sustainability" and "climate anxiety," once confined to academic circles, have entered everyday speech, reflecting a generation that regards environmental crisis as a defining reality.

Older generations have always viewed youthful language with suspicion. In the 18th century, Jonathan Swift complained that the "continual corruption of our English tongue" would lead to cultural decay. Similar lamentations have been heard in every era since. Yet language has not decayed; it has adapted. Each generation's innovations are eventually absorbed into the mainstream, becoming so familiar that their radical origins are forgotten. The word "cool," now universally understood, was once the specialised slang of jazz musicians.

The words we choose matter because they shape how we see the world. Generational language is not a symptom of decline but a sign of a living, adaptive culture. When young people invent new words, they are not destroying language — they are proving that it still works.

## 文章大意

本文探讨代际语言的演变及其文化意义。文章先指出语言是活的系统，俚语是每代人的"语言指纹"；然后回顾从1960年代到今天的青年词汇变迁，分析其驱动因素——技术平台、文化交融和移民社区；再深入分析代际语言反映的世界观变化——数字焦虑、性别认同和气候危机意识；接着指出每代人都批评年轻人的语言，但语言并非在衰退而是在适应；最后强调代际语言不是衰退的症状而是文化活力的证明。
