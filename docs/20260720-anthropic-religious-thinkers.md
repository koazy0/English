# When AI Companies Consult Theologians: Whose Values Get Encoded?

> **Date**: 20260720 · **Source**: Adapted from Scientific American (May 2026) · **Difficulty**: ★★★★☆ · **Type**: 📰 同源外刊精选
>
> **Topic**: #科技 #伦理 #宗教

---

In a development that would have seemed implausible just a few years ago, Anthropic, one of the world's leading AI companies, has begun consulting theologians and ethicists on the moral framework embedded in its language model, Claude. The initiative, first reported in May 2026, involves scholars from multiple religious traditions—Christianity, Islam, Judaism, Buddhism, and secular philosophy—who advise on how the model should respond to questions touching on profound moral and existential concerns.

On its surface, the project appears commendable. If AI systems are going to influence how millions of people think about right and wrong, it seems responsible to seek guidance from those who have studied such questions for millennia. The theologians involved have reportedly shaped Claude's responses on topics ranging from end-of-life care and abortion to grief, forgiveness, and the nature of suffering. In some cases, their input has led to substantive changes—softening the model's previously blunt responses on sensitive issues and introducing greater nuance.

Yet the initiative raises questions that its architects may not have fully anticipated. The most fundamental is: whose values ultimately get encoded? Although the advisory panel represents multiple traditions, it cannot represent all of them. The decision to include certain voices and exclude others is itself an exercise of power—one that inevitably reflects the cultural assumptions of the company making those choices. A theologian from a progressive Protestant denomination and an imam from a conservative Muslim tradition may offer profoundly different guidance on the same question. When Anthropic decides which advice to follow, it is effectively making a theological judgment, even if it frames the decision as balancing perspectives.

This concern becomes especially acute when AI models influence vulnerable individuals. A person grappling with grief who turns to Claude for comfort may receive responses shaped by a particular understanding of suffering that they do not share. A woman considering an abortion may encounter guidance that subtly reflects the moral priorities of whichever tradition prevailed in the model's training. The AI's apparent neutrality—its tone of measured reasonableness—can mask the fact that specific moral commitments have been hard-coded into its responses.

The irony is that Anthropic's effort to be inclusive may paradoxically reinforce certain biases. By selecting a panel of credentialed experts—predominantly from major world religions and Western academic philosophy—the company privileges institutional authority over the lived moral reasoning of ordinary people. A grandmother's wisdom about forgiveness, honed through decades of experience, carries no weight in this framework because it lacks the imprimatur of a doctoral degree.

There is also a question of accountability. When a human therapist offers guidance, they are subject to professional standards, licensing boards, and the possibility of malpractice claims. When an AI model offers guidance shaped by an advisory panel, who is responsible if the advice causes harm? The theologians? The engineers? The company? The current structure diffuses responsibility across so many actors that meaningful accountability becomes elusive.

None of this is to say that consulting ethicists is a bad idea. The alternative—allowing AI companies to make moral decisions entirely behind closed doors—is arguably worse. But the initiative should be understood for what it is: not a solution to the problem of AI alignment, but a reminder of how far we are from solving it. The question is not whether AI should reflect human values. It always will. The question is which values, chosen by whom, and with what accountability.

## 长难句解析

> **When Anthropic decides which advice to follow**, **it is effectively making a theological judgment**, **even if it frames the decision as balancing perspectives**.

- **主干**: it is effectively making a theological judgment
- **When Anthropic decides which advice to follow** 是时间状语从句，which advice to follow 是不定式短语作宾语。
- **even if it frames the decision as balancing perspectives** 是让步状语从句，"即使它将决策框定为平衡各方观点"。
- **翻译**: 当 Anthropic 决定采纳哪些建议时，它实际上是在做出一个神学判断，即使它将这一决策包装成平衡各方观点。

> **The question is not whether AI should reflect human values. It always will. The question is which values, chosen by whom, and with what accountability**.

- 这是三个短句构成的段落，用设问和自答的方式层层推进。
- **which values, chosen by whom, and with what accountability** 三个并列问题，分别追问：哪些价值观、由谁选择、以何种问责机制。
- **翻译**: 问题不在于AI是否应该反映人类价值观。它总会反映的。问题在于：哪些价值观，由谁选择，以及以何种问责机制。

## 文章大意

本文探讨 Anthropic 咨询神学家和伦理学家塑造 Claude 道德框架的做法。表面上值得赞扬，但深层问题是：谁的价值观最终被编码？选择哪些声音参与本身就是权力的行使。当 AI 影响脆弱个体——如悲伤者或考虑堕胎的女性——时，模型的中立语气可能掩盖了被硬编码的特定道德承诺。讽刺的是，选择有资质的专家反而可能强化对制度性权威的偏见。问责也是一个难题：多方参与导致责任扩散。作者认为这不是 AI 对齐问题的解决方案，而是提醒我们离解决问题还有多远。
