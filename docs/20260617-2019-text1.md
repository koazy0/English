## 正文

Guilt has a poor reputation. In popular culture, it is often portrayed as a destructive emotion — something to be overcome, suppressed, or banished altogether. Self-help books promise to help readers "let go of guilt," and therapists routinely encourage their clients to distinguish between productive and unproductive guilt, discarding the latter as a pointless burden. But a growing body of psychological research suggests that this view is incomplete. Guilt, it turns out, may be one of the most socially valuable emotions we possess.

The key insight lies in the relationship between guilt and empathy. Psychologists have long observed that the two emotions frequently appear together: people who feel guilty about causing harm also tend to feel genuine concern for the person they have harmed. This is not a coincidence. Guilt, unlike shame, is inherently relational — it arises from an awareness that one's actions have negatively affected another person. To feel guilty is, by definition, to recognize that someone else has been hurt and that one is responsible for that hurt.

Research by psychologist June Tangney and others has demonstrated that guilt-prone individuals — those who are more susceptible to feelings of guilt — are actually better friends, partners, and citizens than their less guilt-prone counterparts. They are more likely to apologize when they have done something wrong, more likely to take corrective action, and less likely to repeat the offending behavior. In studies of romantic relationships, guilt-prone partners report higher levels of relationship satisfaction — not because they are constantly apologizing, but because they are more attuned to their partner's feelings and more motivated to maintain harmony.

Perhaps most significantly, guilt appears to play a crucial role in moral development. Young children who display a tendency toward guilt are less likely, as they grow older, to engage in antisocial behavior such as lying, cheating, or aggression. The reason is straightforward: guilt acts as an internal brake on selfish impulses. When a child considers taking a toy from a classmate, the anticipated guilt — the awareness that this will cause distress — often prevents the action before it occurs. Over time, this internal regulation becomes a stable feature of personality.

This is not to say that all guilt is beneficial. Excessive, chronic guilt — the kind that persists long after a wrong has been righted, or that arises from situations beyond one's control — can indeed be corrosive. It is associated with depression, anxiety, and a range of other mental health problems. The clinical literature distinguishes carefully between guilt that is proportionate to the offense and guilt that has become detached from any reasonable moral calculus.

The challenge, then, is not to eliminate guilt but to calibrate it. A society in which nobody felt guilt would be a society without moral restraint. A society in which everyone felt too much of it would be paralyzed by anxiety. The healthiest approach lies somewhere in between — recognizing guilt as a signal that we have fallen short of our own standards and using it as a prompt to do better, rather than allowing it to become a permanent weight.

## 文章大意

本文重新审视了愧疚情绪的价值。虽然愧疚在流行文化中被视为负面情绪，但心理学研究表明愧疚与共情密切相关，易愧疚的人往往是更好的朋友、伴侣和公民。愧疚在道德发展中也起关键作用，作为自私冲动的内在刹车。但过度、慢性的愧疚确实有害。作者认为关键不是消除愧疚而是校准它——将愧疚视为改进自我的信号而非永久负担。

   - A) Guilt is always more intense than shame
   - B) Guilt is inherently relational, arising from awareness of harm to others
   - C) Shame is a more useful emotion than guilt
   - D) Guilt is an emotion only experienced by children

2. What role does guilt play in moral development according to the research?
   - A) It makes children more aggressive
   - B) It acts as an internal brake on selfish impulses
   - C) It prevents children from forming friendships
   - D) It has no significant effect on behavior

3. What does the author suggest is the healthiest approach to guilt?
   - A) Eliminating all feelings of guilt
   - B) Embracing guilt as a permanent state of mind
   - C) Calibrating guilt — using it as a signal to improve, not a permanent burden
   - D) Replacing guilt entirely with empathy
