## 正文

Where do new ideas come from? It is a question that has fascinated philosophers, artists, and scientists for centuries. The ancient Greeks attributed creative inspiration to the Muses; the Romantics saw it as a mysterious force flowing from the soul. Modern neuroscience offers a less romantic but arguably more useful answer: creativity arises from the brain's ability to combine existing concepts in novel ways, and understanding this process can help us become more creative.

The brain does not create ideas from nothing. Instead, it draws on vast stores of accumulated knowledge and experience, searching for connections between concepts that are not obviously related. This process, which neuroscientists call "associative thinking," relies heavily on the brain's default mode network — a collection of brain regions that become active when we are not focused on a specific task. Daydreaming, mind-wandering, and the relaxed state just before sleep are all associated with heightened activity in this network. It is no coincidence that many of history's great ideas — from Archimedes in his bath to Newton under his apple tree — arrived during moments of apparent idleness.

Associative thinking works by activating conceptually distant nodes in the brain's semantic network. Imagine the mind as a vast web of interconnected ideas. In most daily thinking, we travel along well-worn paths — connecting "coffee" with "morning" or "meeting" with "office." Creative thinking requires leaping to more distant connections — linking "coffee" with "revolution" (coffeehouses as centres of political debate) or "meeting" with "river confluence" (the original meaning of the word). The more diverse our knowledge and experience, the richer the web of possible connections.

This understanding helps explain why certain conditions foster creativity. Diverse experiences — living abroad, learning new skills, reading widely — enrich the semantic network, providing more raw material for novel associations. Psychological distance — imagining a problem as if it were far away in time or space — has been shown to promote creative thinking by reducing the mental constraints of immediate context. Even physical movement helps: walking increases creative output by roughly 60 percent, according to a Stanford University study, presumably because mild exercise promotes divergent thinking.

But can creativity be trained? The evidence suggests it can — up to a point. Creative training programmes that teach techniques such as analogical reasoning, perspective-taking, and constraint relaxation have shown modest but measurable improvements in divergent thinking tasks. Importantly, these techniques do not bestow talent where none exists; rather, they help individuals access and develop the creative capacity they already possess.

There are, however, limits to this approach. Truly revolutionary ideas — Darwin's theory of evolution, Einstein's relativity — require not just novel associations but deep domain expertise and years of sustained effort. Creativity is not merely a cognitive trick; it is the product of knowledge, effort, and the willingness to pursue connections that others dismiss. Understanding the neuroscience of creativity does not make genius commonplace, but it does suggest that most people are more creative than they realise — if they create the right conditions for their ideas to emerge.

## 文章大意

本文从神经科学角度探讨创造力的机制。文章先否定创造力来自虚无或神灵，指出其本质是大脑将现有概念以新方式组合；然后介绍联想思维和默认模式网络——大脑在放松时更容易产生远程联想；再解释创造力为何需要概念上的"远跳"和多样化的知识储备；接着分析促进创造力的条件（多元经历、心理距离、身体运动）和创造力训练的可能性（类比推理、换位思考等技巧有适度效果）；最后指出创造力训练的局限——真正的革命性想法需要深厚的领域专业知识和持续努力，但大多数人比自己认为的更有创造力。
