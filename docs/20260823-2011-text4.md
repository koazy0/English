## 正文

For most of human history, war was a relatively straightforward affair. Armies marched across borders, battles were fought on defined terrain, and the side with superior force usually prevailed. The invention of gunpowder, the machine gun, and the atomic bomb each transformed the nature of conflict, but the essential structure — states fighting states with organised military forces — remained recognisable. In the 21st century, this familiar pattern has fractured beyond recognition.

The most dramatic change is the rise of cyber warfare. Nations can now attack each other without firing a single shot. In 2010, the Stuxnet computer worm — widely attributed to the United States and Israel — disabled Iranian nuclear centrifuges, setting back the country's nuclear programme by years. This was not espionage in the traditional sense; it was an act of physical destruction achieved entirely through digital means. Since then, major cyberattacks have targeted electrical grids, banking systems, and government databases around the world. A determined attacker can disrupt a nation's infrastructure without ever crossing its borders.

Drones have transformed the battlefield in equally profound ways. Unmanned aerial vehicles allow nations to conduct surveillance and launch precision strikes without putting their own pilots at risk. The United States has carried out thousands of drone strikes in Pakistan, Yemen, and Somalia, killing both militant leaders and, controversially, civilians who happened to be nearby. The ease of remote warfare lowers the political threshold for using military force — when no soldiers are at risk, the decision to strike becomes easier.

Asymmetric warfare has further blurred the lines of conflict. Non-state actors — terrorist organisations, insurgent groups, private military companies — can now wield destructive capabilities that were once the exclusive domain of nation-states. A small group with access to the internet can disrupt critical infrastructure, spread disinformation, or coordinate attacks across borders. The traditional advantage of large, well-funded militaries is eroded when a handful of determined individuals can cause disproportionate damage.

These changes pose fundamental challenges to the laws and norms that have governed armed conflict for generations. International humanitarian law was designed for wars between states, with clear distinctions between combatants and civilians, and between acts of war and acts of crime. In cyberspace, these distinctions collapse. A hacker sitting in a civilian building in one country can launch an attack that disables a hospital in another. Is this an act of war, a crime, or something in between? The existing legal framework provides no clear answer.

The transformation of war also raises deeper questions about accountability and democratic oversight. When a drone strike is ordered from a base thousands of miles away, or when a cyberattack is launched from within a government agency with minimal public scrutiny, who bears responsibility? The traditional mechanisms of democratic accountability — parliamentary debate, public opinion, media scrutiny — were designed for conflicts that are visible and debatable. The new warfare is often invisible, classified, and conducted with minimal transparency.

War has not ended; it has mutated. The challenge for the international community is to develop new rules, norms, and institutions capable of managing these new forms of conflict before they cause the very catastrophes they were designed to prevent.

## 文章大意

本文讨论现代战争形态的深刻变革。文章先回顾传统战争模式的演变，指出21世纪战争模式已面目全非；然后逐一分析三种新形态：网络战（Stuxnet等数字攻击造成物理破坏）、无人机战（远程打击降低使用武力的政治门槛）、非对称战争（非国家行为体获得巨大破坏力）；接着指出这些变化使国际人道主义法律（为传统国与国战争设计）的适用性面临挑战——战斗员与平民、战争行为与犯罪行为的界限模糊了；再讨论新战争形态下民主问责的困难（不可见、机密化、缺乏透明度）；最后呼吁国际社会制定新的规则和制度。
