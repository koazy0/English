## 正文

The numbers are alarming. Rates of depression and anxiety among teenagers in developed countries have risen sharply over the past decade, with some studies reporting a doubling of symptoms since 2010. During the same period, smartphone ownership among adolescents has climbed from roughly 20 percent to over 90 percent. The correlation is striking — and it has prompted a fierce debate among researchers, parents, and policymakers about whether screen time is causing a mental health crisis among the young.

Those who see a causal link point to several mechanisms. Social media, they argue, exposes young people to relentless social comparison. Teenagers can now measure their popularity in real time through likes, followers, and comments — a form of constant evaluation that previous generations never faced. Cyberbullying, which can follow a child home from school and into the bedroom, is far harder to escape than traditional bullying. The blue light emitted by screens disrupts sleep patterns, and poor sleep is itself a risk factor for depression. The evidence, advocates of this view contend, is not merely correlational: longitudinal studies have found that increases in screen time precede increases in depressive symptoms.

Sceptics are not convinced. They point out that correlation is not causation and that many other factors could explain rising rates of adolescent distress. Economic insecurity following the 2008 financial crisis, heightened academic pressure, climate anxiety, and the decline of face-to-face community have all been proposed as alternative explanations. Some researchers note that the effect sizes in studies linking screen time to depression are small — comparable, one leading psychologist observed, to the effect of regularly eating potatoes. The researchers who produced that comparison were widely criticised, but the underlying point stands: the statistical relationship between screens and distress is weaker than public discussion often suggests.

The debate is further complicated by the fact that "screen time" is a hopelessly blunt instrument. An hour spent video-chatting with a grandparent, an hour spent researching a school project, and an hour spent scrolling through idealised images on Instagram are all counted as "screen time" — yet their psychological effects are likely to be very different. Content matters more than duration, and active use (creating, communicating) generally has different effects from passive consumption (scrolling, viewing).

There is also the question of directionality. It may be that screen time causes depression, but it is equally possible that depressed teenagers spend more time on screens — withdrawing from social activities they once enjoyed and seeking refuge in the private world of a smartphone. Most studies are not designed to distinguish cause from effect.

What, then, should parents and policymakers do in the face of this uncertainty? The prudent approach is to apply the precautionary principle. While the evidence for a direct causal link remains contested, there is broad agreement that excessive, passive, and late-night screen use is associated with poorer mental health. Encouraging reasonable limits, protecting sleep, and promoting active over passive use are steps that carry little risk and potentially significant benefit. At the same time, researchers must develop more refined measures that move beyond "screen time" to examine what young people are actually doing with their devices. Only then can we move from correlation to understanding — and from understanding to effective action.

## 文章大意

本文探讨屏幕时间与青少年抑郁之间的关系。文章先展示青少年抑郁上升与智能手机普及的相关性，引出因果之争；然后概述"有因果"方的论据——社交比较、网络霸凌、睡眠干扰和纵向研究证据；再介绍"怀疑"方的反驳——相关性不等于因果、效应量小、其他解释因素；接着指出"屏幕时间"概念过于粗糙（内容比时长更重要）和因果关系方向性的问题；最后提出谨慎原则——在证据尚不确定时采取合理限制措施，同时呼吁研究更精细的测量方法。

## 思考题

**1.** What argument do those who believe screen time causes depression make about longitudinal studies?

A. These studies show that screen time has no effect on mental health
B. These studies demonstrate that depression leads to increased screen time
C. These studies find that increases in screen time come before increases in depression
D. These studies prove that only social media use affects teenagers

<details><summary>答案</summary>C。文章第二段指出纵向研究发现"increases in screen time precede increases in depressive symptoms"，即屏幕时间增加先于抑郁症状增加，这是因果论的重要证据。</details>

**2.** Why does the author consider "screen time" a "hopelessly blunt instrument"?

A. Because it does not distinguish between different types of screen activities
B. Because it only measures time spent on smartphones, not other devices
C. Because it fails to count offline activities that affect mental health
D. Because it has been rejected by most researchers as a valid measure

<details><summary>答案</summary>A。文章第四段指出不同屏幕活动（视频聊天、做研究、刷Instagram）都被算作"屏幕时间"，但"their psychological effects are likely to be very different"，说明这个概念不区分活动类型。</details>

**3.** What does the author recommend while the debate remains unresolved?

A. Banning all smartphone use among teenagers
B. Waiting until conclusive evidence is available before taking any action
C. Applying the precautionary principle with reasonable limits on screen use
D. Encouraging teenagers to decide their own screen time limits

<details><summary>答案</summary>C。文章末段建议"apply the precautionary principle"，即采取合理限制、保护睡眠、鼓励主动而非被动使用屏幕——这些措施风险小而潜在收益大。</details>
