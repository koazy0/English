## 正文

In the autumn of 2012, schools and businesses across Europe began to notice an alarming shortage of a seemingly ordinary commodity: paper. Printers ran out of supplies, textbook deliveries were delayed, and office suppliers posted notices apologizing for the inconvenience. The culprit, it soon emerged, was not a natural disaster or a supply chain failure but something far more deliberate — the exercise of monopoly power.

For decades, the European paper industry had been consolidating. Smaller mills were acquired by larger corporations, and larger corporations merged with one another, until a single company controlled a dominant share of the market for certain types of paper. With competitors eliminated, the company was free to reduce production and raise prices at will. The result was precisely what economists would predict: artificial scarcity, inflated costs, and widespread frustration among consumers.

Monopoly, as economists have long understood, distorts markets in predictable ways. When a single firm controls the supply of a product, it has no incentive to innovate, to improve quality, or to keep prices low. Competition, by contrast, forces firms to operate efficiently and to pass savings on to consumers. Without it, the monopolist can simply restrict output and watch profits rise, regardless of the consequences for everyone else.

The paper industry is not unique in this regard. Similar patterns have been observed in industries ranging from pharmaceuticals to telecommunications. When a single company controls a crucial resource — whether it is a drug patent, a communications network, or a raw material — the incentive to exploit that control for maximum profit is often overwhelming. The costs are borne not only by direct consumers but by society at large, in the form of higher prices, reduced innovation, and constrained economic growth.

Governments have tried various strategies to address the problem. Antitrust laws exist in most developed economies, and regulatory agencies are empowered to break up monopolies or block mergers that threaten competition. In practice, however, enforcement is often slow and ineffective. By the time regulators act, the damage may already be done — businesses have closed, consumers have paid inflated prices, and the competitive landscape has been permanently altered.

Some economists argue that the solution lies not in breaking up monopolies after the fact but in preventing them from forming in the first place. Stricter merger review processes, they suggest, would reduce the likelihood of any single firm gaining dominant market share. Others counter that excessive regulation stifles innovation and that large firms can achieve economies of scale that benefit consumers.

What the paper shortage illustrates, above all, is that monopoly is not merely an abstract economic concept. It has real and tangible consequences for ordinary people — from the teacher who cannot print worksheets to the small business that cannot afford its supply costs. Ensuring competitive markets, then, is not just a matter of economic theory; it is a matter of basic fairness.

## 长难句解析

### 句子1

> With competitors eliminated, the company was free to reduce production and raise prices at will, and the result was precisely what economists would predict: artificial scarcity, inflated costs, and widespread frustration.

**拆分**：
- **独立主格结构**: With competitors eliminated（竞争对手被消除后）
- **分句1**: the company was free to reduce production and raise prices at will（公司可以随意减少产量和提高价格）
- **分句2**: the result was precisely what economists would predict（结果正是经济学家所预测的）
  - what economists would predict 是表语从句
- **同位语**: artificial scarcity, inflated costs, and widespread frustration（对结果的解释）

**翻译**: 随着竞争对手被消除，公司可以随意减少产量和提高价格，结果正是经济学家所预测的：人为短缺、膨胀的成本和普遍的沮丧。

### 句子2

> When a single company controls a crucial resource — whether it is a drug patent, a communications network, or a raw material — the incentive to exploit that control for maximum profit is often overwhelming.

**拆分**：
- **时间状语从句**: When a single company controls a crucial resource（当一家公司控制了关键资源时）
- **插入语**: whether it is a drug patent, a communications network, or a raw material（无论是药品专利、通信网络还是原材料）
- **主句**: the incentive to exploit that control for maximum profit is often overwhelming（利用这种控制获取最大利润的动机往往是压倒性的）

**翻译**: 当一家公司控制了关键资源——无论是药品专利、通信网络还是原材料——利用这种控制获取最大利润的动机往往是压倒性的。

## 文章大意

本文以2012年欧洲纸张短缺事件为例，讨论了垄断对市场的危害。行业合并导致单一公司主导市场，人为制造短缺并抬高价格。文章指出垄断会扼杀创新、损害消费者利益，虽然反垄断法律存在但执行缓慢，而纸张短缺事件表明垄断对普通人有切实的影响，维护竞争市场不仅是经济理论问题，更是基本公平的问题。

## 思考题

1. What was the primary cause of the paper shortage in Europe in 2012?
   - A) A natural disaster disrupted supply chains
   - B) A single company used its dominant market position to restrict supply
   - C) Consumer demand for paper increased dramatically
   - D) Paper mills were forced to close due to environmental regulations

2. According to the passage, why is antitrust enforcement often ineffective?
   - A) Regulators lack the legal authority to act
   - B) Enforcement is typically too slow to prevent damage
   - C) Companies ignore regulatory orders entirely
   - D) Consumers prefer monopolies because they offer lower prices

3. The author's main argument in the final paragraph is that:
   - A) Monopoly is an abstract concept with little real-world impact
   - B) The paper industry should be nationalized
   - C) Monopoly has concrete consequences that affect ordinary people
   - D) Economists disagree about whether monopolies are harmful

<details>
<summary>参考答案</summary>

1. **B** — 文中明确指出"the exercise of monopoly power"是纸张短缺的原因，单一公司控制市场后减少产量、提高价格。

2. **B** — 文中指出"enforcement is often slow and ineffective"，到监管者采取行动时，损害已经造成（"the damage may already be done"）。

3. **C** — 末段强调垄断"has real and tangible consequences for ordinary people"，并以教师和小企业为例说明垄断对普通人的具体影响。
