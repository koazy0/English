## 正文

A decade ago, technology companies were celebrated as the vanguard of innovation. Today, they are more often described as monopolists. Google controls roughly 90 percent of the search market. Amazon accounts for nearly 40 percent of all online retail in the United States. Facebook — now Meta — owns three of the world's six largest social platforms. Apple dictates the terms of its App Store, taking commissions of up to 30 percent from developers. The question confronting policymakers is no longer whether Big Tech has too much power, but what to do about it.

The case for action rests on multiple grounds. First, market dominance allows platforms to extract excessive rents. Apple's App Store commissions, for instance, are significantly higher than what a competitive market would sustain. Second, dominant platforms can stifle competition by acquiring potential rivals before they become threats. Facebook's purchases of Instagram and WhatsApp eliminated two nascent competitors that might have challenged its social media supremacy. Third, platforms that operate both a marketplace and compete within it can exploit their position. Amazon has repeatedly been accused of using data from third-party sellers on its platform to launch competing products of its own, undercutting the very businesses that depend on its marketplace.

The harms extend beyond economics. When a handful of companies control the channels through which billions of people communicate, access information, and form opinions, the implications for democracy are profound. Algorithmic curation determines what news people see, what ideas gain traction, and which voices are amplified or silenced. The concentration of such power in unelected, unaccountable corporations is without precedent in modern history.

The debate over remedies is fierce. One camp advocates breaking up the largest companies — separating Amazon's retail operations from its cloud business, or spinning off Instagram from Facebook. They draw inspiration from the breakup of Standard Oil in 1911 and AT&T in 1984, arguing that structural separation eliminates conflicts of interest and restores competition. A second camp favours regulation over breakup, arguing that breaking up tech companies would sacrifice economies of scale and network benefits that consumers value. Instead, they propose treating platforms as essential infrastructure — like utilities — subject to rules about access, pricing, and non-discrimination. A third camp argues that neither breakup nor regulation addresses the root problem: the business model itself. As long as companies profit from attention and data extraction, they will find ways to exploit users.

Each approach has strengths and weaknesses, and the most effective solution may combine elements of all three. What is certain is that the status quo is unsustainable. Antitrust law, last updated in a pre-digital era, is poorly equipped to address the dynamics of platform markets, where network effects create winner-take-all outcomes and where the product — the user — has no price. Reforming competition policy for the digital age is not anti-innovation; it is the prerequisite for ensuring that innovation serves the many, not the few.

## 长难句解析

**1.** Amazon has repeatedly been accused of using data from third-party sellers on its platform to launch competing products of its own, undercutting the very businesses that depend on its marketplace.

- **拆分**：主句为 Amazon has been accused of using data...to launch...，现在分词 undercutting 作结果状语；the very businesses 中 very 表强调，that 引导定语从句。
- **翻译**：亚马逊一再被指控利用其平台上第三方卖家的数据来推出自有竞争产品，从而削弱了那些依赖其市场的商家。

**2.** Antitrust law, last updated in a pre-digital era, is poorly equipped to address the dynamics of platform markets, where network effects create winner-take-all outcomes and where the product — the user — has no price.

- **拆分**：主语 antitrust law，谓语 is poorly equipped to address；where 引导两个并列定语从句修饰 platform markets；the user 为 the product 的同位语。
- **翻译**：反垄断法最后一次更新还在数字时代之前，难以应对平台市场的动态，在那里网络效应造成赢者通吃的局面，而产品——即用户——没有价格。

## 文章大意

本文分析了科技巨头的垄断问题及应对之策。谷歌、亚马逊、Facebook、苹果各自在搜索、电商、社交和应用分发领域占据主导地位。垄断的危害包括：收取过高佣金、收购潜在竞争对手、利用平台数据自营竞争。更深层的问题在于少数公司控制了数十亿人的信息渠道，对民主构成威胁。关于解决方案，三派观点分别是：拆分公司（如标准石油和AT&T的先例）、将平台视为基础设施进行监管、或改变注意力经济和数据榨取的商业模式本身。作者认为可能需要综合方案，并强调改革竞争政策是创新服务大众的前提。
