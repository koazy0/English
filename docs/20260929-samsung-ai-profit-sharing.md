# Samsung Memory Chip Staff Get £310,000 Bonuses After AI Profit-Sharing Deal

---

When Samsung Electronics announced that its memory chip division employees would receive bonuses equivalent to £310,000 per person, the news landed with a mixture of awe and unease. The bonuses, among the largest ever paid to non-executive employees in the semiconductor industry, reflect the staggering profits generated by the artificial intelligence boom — and the uncomfortable reality that the wealth created by AI is being distributed with a selectivity that mirrors the technology's own patterns of concentration.

The profit-sharing arrangement stems from Samsung's record-breaking financial year, driven primarily by demand for high-bandwidth memory (HBM) chips that are essential for training and running large AI models. Nvidia's GPUs, the hardware backbone of the AI revolution, require HBM chips to function, and Samsung is one of only three companies worldwide — alongside SK Hynix and Micron — capable of manufacturing them at scale. The resulting supply-demand imbalance has sent chip prices soaring, and Samsung's semiconductor division posted operating profits that exceeded the GDP of several small nations.

The bonuses are structured through Samsung's OPI (Overall Profit Incentive) program, which allocates up to 20 percent of division profits to employees who have worked in the division for more than one year. The £310,000 figure represents the maximum payout for senior engineers and researchers; production line workers receive substantially less, though still life-changing amounts by South Korean standards. The program reflects a corporate culture that, while hardly egalitarian, recognizes that retaining engineering talent in a fiercely competitive market requires sharing the upside.

The contrast with other industries affected by AI could not be starker. While Samsung's chip engineers receive six-figure bonuses, the content creators whose work is scraped to train AI models receive nothing. The data center workers who maintain the physical infrastructure of AI are often employed on temporary contracts without profit-sharing. The gig workers who label training data earn pennies per task. The AI value chain is generating enormous wealth, but it is concentrated at a remarkably small number of points — chip designers, cloud providers, and AI model companies — while the broader ecosystem captures little of the value it helps create.

South Korean labor advocates have noted that even within Samsung, the distribution raises questions. Production workers, who endure some of the most grueling conditions in the semiconductor industry — clean room shifts lasting 12 hours, exposure to hazardous chemicals, physically demanding repetitive tasks — receive bonuses that, while generous, are a fraction of what engineers and researchers take home. The AI boom has not eliminated the hierarchy between intellectual and manual labor; it has amplified it.
