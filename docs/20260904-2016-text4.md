# 2016 Text 4 — 人口老龄化对社会的影响

## 正文

The world is growing old at an unprecedented rate. By 2050, the number of people aged 65 and over will double, reaching 1.5 billion globally. In Japan, already the world's oldest society, more than 29 percent of the population is over 65. Europe is not far behind, and even China — long the emblem of youthful dynamism — is ageing faster than any country in history. This demographic shift is not merely a statistical curiosity; it is reshaping economies, straining social systems, and forcing a fundamental rethink of how societies organise themselves.

The fiscal consequences are staggering. Pension costs are rising sharply as fewer workers support more retirees. In many European countries, the ratio of workers to pensioners has fallen from five to one in the 1970s to barely two to one today. Healthcare expenditures are climbing even faster, because older adults typically require two to three times more medical care than younger populations. In Japan, social security spending now consumes over a third of the national budget, crowding out investment in education and infrastructure.

The labour market is also under pressure. A shrinking workforce means slower economic growth, fewer taxpayers, and greater difficulty filling essential jobs. Japan, with its chronic labour shortages, has already begun relaxing immigration rules and deploying robots in nursing homes. Germany has raised its retirement age to 67 and is encouraging more women and older workers into employment. Yet these measures, while helpful, can only partially offset the effects of a declining population.

Beyond economics, ageing societies face profound social challenges. Loneliness among the elderly has reached epidemic levels. In the United Kingdom, more than a million older people say they regularly go a month without speaking to a friend, neighbour, or family member. The burden of eldercare falls disproportionately on women, many of whom reduce their working hours or leave employment entirely to care for ageing relatives. Meanwhile, intergenerational tensions are growing, as younger people resent the taxes they pay to support a system from which they feel they will never benefit.

Japan offers both a warning and a glimpse of possible solutions. The country has invested heavily in age-friendly infrastructure, from barrier-free housing to community buses that run on demand. It has pioneered the concept of the "lifelong learning society," encouraging older adults to remain active and engaged. Some Japanese companies have redesigned workplaces to accommodate older employees, with shorter shifts and ergonomic equipment. Yet even Japan has not solved the fundamental problem: how to pay for growing social commitments with a shrinking tax base.

The lesson is clear. Ageing is not a crisis that will pass; it is a permanent transformation that demands permanent adjustments. Countries that act now — by reforming pension systems, investing in preventive healthcare, and redesigning cities for older residents — will fare far better than those that delay. The demographic clock is ticking, and it will not wait.

## 长难句解析

**1.** In many European countries, the ratio of workers to pensioners has fallen from five to one in the 1970s to barely two to one today.

- **拆分**：主语 the ratio of workers to pensioners，谓语 has fallen，from...to... 表示比率的下降变化，两个 to 含义不同：to one 表示比例，to barely 表示变化方向。
- **翻译**：在许多欧洲国家，劳动者与退休人员的比例已从20世纪70年代的5:1下降到如今勉强2:1。

**2.** Countries that act now — by reforming pension systems, investing in preventive healthcare, and redesigning cities for older residents — will fare far better than those that delay.

- **拆分**：that act now 为定语从句修饰 countries；破折号之间三个动名词短语解释 act now 的具体方式；those that delay 中 that delay 为定语从句。
- **翻译**：现在就采取行动的国家——通过改革养老金制度、投资预防性医疗保健、为老年居民重新设计城市——将比那些拖延的国家过得好得多。

## 文章大意

本文分析了全球人口老龄化带来的深远影响。到2050年，65岁以上人口将翻倍达到15亿，日本已成为全球最老龄化社会。财政后果令人震惊：养老金和医疗支出急剧上升，劳动者与退休人员比例下降。劳动力市场也面临萎缩，经济增长放缓。社会层面，老年人孤独问题严重，照护负担不成比例地落在女性身上，代际矛盾加剧。日本的经验既有警示也有启示：投资适老化基础设施、推动终身学习、重新设计工作场所。文章强调，老龄化不是暂时的危机，而是需要永久性调整的持久性变革。
