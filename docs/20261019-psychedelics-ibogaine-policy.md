# Psychedelics, Ibogaine, and the Republican Pushing for It

---

In the landscape of American politics, few alliances are as unlikely as the one forming around ibogaine, a powerful psychedelic derived from the root bark of an African shrub, traditionally used in spiritual ceremonies in West Central Africa. The substance, which has shown remarkable promise in treating opioid addiction, post-traumatic stress disorder, and depression, is being championed by a growing coalition that includes progressive Democrats, libertarian-minded Republicans, military veterans, and — most surprisingly — a conservative Republican congressman from Texas who has built his career on law-and-order politics.

Representative Dan Crenshaw, a former Navy SEAL who lost an eye in Afghanistan, is not the sort of politician one would expect to advocate for a Schedule I substance. He has voted against marijuana decriminalization, supported mandatory minimum sentences, and built his public persona on an unflinching toughness. But Crenshaw's advocacy for ibogaine is driven by an experience that transcends political categories: the opioid crisis is killing his constituents and his fellow veterans at a rate that makes ideological purity an unaffordable luxury.

The numbers are staggering. In 2023, over 107,000 Americans died of drug overdoses, the majority involving synthetic opioids like fentanyl. Among veterans, the suicide rate is 57 percent higher than the general population, and many of those deaths are opioid-related. Traditional treatment — methadone, buprenorphine, counseling — has a success rate of roughly 30 percent at best. Ibogaine, in the limited clinical studies that have been conducted, shows a single-treatment success rate of between 50 and 80 percent for opioid addiction, a figure so dramatically superior to existing treatments that it demands attention regardless of one's feelings about psychedelics.

The science, while preliminary, is compelling. Ibogaine appears to work through a mechanism unlike any existing addiction treatment. Rather than merely substituting one opioid for another, as methadone does, ibogaine seems to reset the brain's addiction pathways, reducing withdrawal symptoms and cravings in a single session. Functional MRI studies show changes in brain connectivity patterns that persist for months after treatment. The experience is often described as one of confronting the root causes of addiction — trauma, grief, alienation — rather than merely suppressing its symptoms.

The opposition comes from predictable and not-so-predictable quarters. The pharmaceutical industry, which profits from maintenance therapies that require daily dosing for life, has lobbied against ibogaine research funding. Some addiction specialists warn that the substance carries cardiac risks, pointing to a small number of deaths during or shortly after treatment. And cultural conservatives object on principle to the idea of treating addiction with a substance that produces hallucinogenic experiences.

What makes the ibogaine debate different from previous drug policy disputes is the composition of its supporters. When veterans' organizations, conservative politicians, and progressive activists find themselves on the same side, the traditional framing of drug policy as a left-right issue collapses. The question becomes not whether psychedelics are culturally acceptable but whether the existing treatment paradigm is morally defensible when a dramatically more effective alternative exists but remains legally inaccessible.

## 文章大意

伊博加因（ibogaine）——一种非洲灌木根皮提取的强效迷幻剂——在治疗阿片成瘾方面展现出50-80%的单次治疗成功率，远超现有疗法的30%。其支持者联盟极为罕见：进步派民主党人、自由意志主义共和党人、退伍军人和以强硬著称的德州保守派议员Dan Crenshaw。Crenshaw的倡导源于选区和退伍军人中阿片危机的惨烈现实。制药行业、部分成瘾专家和文化保守派反对伊博加因，但当退伍军人和保守政治家与进步活动家站在一起时，药物政策的传统左右框架已无法维持。
