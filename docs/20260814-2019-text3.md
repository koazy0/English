## 正文

In 2016, Google Translate underwent a transformation. The system, which had previously relied on phrase-based statistical methods, was rebuilt using deep neural networks. Almost overnight, the quality of its translations improved dramatically. Sentences that had once been garbled became coherent; passages that required painstaking human correction became passable with a single click. The age of machine translation, long predicted, had finally arrived — and with it, a question that strikes at the heart of what language means.

The progress since then has been remarkable. Today's translation systems handle dozens of languages with impressive fluency. Business travellers use smartphone apps to negotiate meetings in real time. Researchers can scan foreign-language papers without waiting for human translators. Social media platforms automatically translate posts, enabling conversations across linguistic boundaries that would have been impossible a decade ago. For practical purposes, the language barrier is lower than it has ever been.

Yet professional translators are not obsolete — and the reasons why reveal important limitations of machine translation. The first is context. A single word can carry radically different meanings depending on the surrounding text, the speaker's intent, and the cultural setting. The English word "right" can mean a direction, a moral entitlement, or a statement of correctness. Humans resolve such ambiguities effortlessly by drawing on world knowledge and social understanding; machines, despite their vast data, still struggle.

The second limitation is tone and style. Legal documents, poetry, diplomatic correspondence, and literary fiction each demand a distinct register that conveys not just meaning but nuance, emotion, and cultural resonance. When a Chinese poet writes of "月落乌啼霜满天" — the moon setting, crows cawing, frost filling the sky — a machine translation may capture the literal meaning but loses the evocative rhythm and centuries of cultural allusion that give the line its power. Translation is not merely the transfer of information; it is the recreation of experience.

The third concern is more fundamental: what is lost when we rely on machines to bridge linguistic divides? Language is not just a tool for communication but a carrier of identity, memory, and worldview. Each language encodes unique ways of perceiving reality. The Yupik people of Alaska have dozens of words for snow, each describing a distinct condition vital to survival. When translation becomes effortless, the incentive to learn other languages diminishes — and with it, the deep cultural understanding that language learning brings.

None of this is to deny the benefits of AI translation. In emergencies, in commerce, in everyday communication across borders, it is a gift. But we should be clear-eyed about what it offers and what it does not. Machine translation transfers information; human translation transmits understanding. Both are valuable, but they are not the same thing — and confusing them risks losing something that no algorithm can restore.

## 文章大意

本文讨论AI翻译的进步与局限。文章先描述2016年Google Translate的飞跃性改进及翻译质量的提升；然后肯定机器翻译的实际便利性；接着从三个方面分析其局限：语境理解（一词多义需要世界知识）、语调风格（法律文书和文学诗歌需要传递细微差别和文化共鸣）、语言学习的消减效应（翻译便捷使学习外语的动机降低，从而丧失深层文化理解）；最后区分机器翻译传递信息与人工翻译传递理解，两者虽都有价值但不可混为一谈。
