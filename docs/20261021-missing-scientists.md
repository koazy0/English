# Missing Scientists: The Erosion of Scientific Expertise in Public Institutions

| 项目 | 内容 |
|------|------|
| 日期 | 2026-10-21 |
| 类型 | 外刊精选 |
| 难度 | ★★★★☆ |
| 来源 | The Atlantic |

---

Something is disappearing from the federal government, and its absence is being felt in ways that are difficult to quantify but impossible to ignore. Scientists — experienced, senior, career researchers who have spent decades building expertise in environmental monitoring, disease surveillance, food safety, and climate modeling — are leaving public service at rates that have accelerated dramatically since 2020. The exodus is not a single event but a slow erosion, and its consequences will accumulate for years before they become fully visible.

The causes are multiple and mutually reinforcing. Budget cuts to federal research agencies have reduced the resources available for scientific work, even as the demands on those agencies have grown. Political interference in scientific findings — from the suppression of climate reports during the Trump administration to the muzzling of pandemic researchers during COVID — has damaged morale among career scientists who entered government service precisely because they believed in the integrity of evidence-based policy. And the private sector, particularly the technology and pharmaceutical industries, offers salaries two to five times higher than federal pay scales for researchers with comparable expertise.

The numbers tell a stark story. The Environmental Protection Agency has lost approximately 16 percent of its scientific staff since 2016. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has experienced a similar attrition rate, with senior epidemiologists and laboratory scientists departing at the highest rates. The National Weather Service, already understaffed, has seen vacancy rates in critical forecasting positions exceed 20 percent. These are not administrative positions that can be filled quickly; they represent decades of institutional knowledge that cannot be replaced by hiring replacements.

The consequences are already materializing. The Government Accountability Office has documented multiple instances where the absence of senior scientific staff contributed to delayed or inadequate responses to environmental and public health crises. Water quality monitoring in rural areas has been reduced in scope. The timeline for reviewing new chemicals under the Toxic Substances Control Act has lengthened significantly. The nation's weather forecasting models, which depend on continuous refinement by experienced meteorologists, have fallen behind European models in accuracy for the first time in decades.

Perhaps most concerning is the loss of what might be called institutional courage — the willingness of career scientists to speak truth to power based on their expertise and the protection of their professional standing. When senior scientists depart, they take with them not only their knowledge but also their ability to resist political pressure. Junior staff, who lack the professional authority and job security of their senior colleagues, are less likely to challenge directives that contradict scientific evidence. The result is a government that is not merely less knowledgeable but less honest.

Rebuilding this capacity will take far longer than destroying it. Training a senior environmental toxicologist or a career epidemiologist requires a decade of education followed by years of practical experience. The pipeline is long, and the generation of scientists who might have filled it has been diverted to more lucrative and less politically volatile employment. A government that has driven away its scientific expertise may discover that it needs that expertise at exactly the moment when it is no longer available.

## 文章大意

美国政府资深科学家正在大量离职，构成难以量化但不可忽视的流失。原因包括预算削减、政治干预损害士气，以及私营部门薪资远高于联邦水平。EPA和CDC各流失约16%的科研人员，国家气象局关键岗位空缺率超过20%。后果已经显现：水质监测范围缩小、化学品审查时间延长、天气预报准确率首次落后欧洲。最令人担忧的是"制度勇气"的丧失——资深科学家离岗后，初级员工更难抵制政治压力。重建能力远比摧毁它耗时更长。
