# The Little Gen Z Midterm Election

---

The 2026 midterm elections were supposed to be a referendum on the economy. Instead, they became something more interesting: a quiet rebellion by the youngest voters in the American electorate, whose political behavior is confounding every model built to predict it. Generation Z — those born between 1997 and 2012 — turned out in numbers that stunned pollsters, but they did not vote the way either party expected. They voted as if the categories that organized American politics for decades no longer applied to them, because, in many cases, they do not.

The defining economic experience of Gen Z is not recession but precarity. They came of age during a pandemic that erased entry-level jobs, entered a housing market where the median home price had decoupled from the median income, and accumulated student debt that their parents' generation did not face at comparable ages. But precarity does not map neatly onto the traditional left-right political axis. It produces not consistent liberalism but a volatile, issue-specific engagement that cuts across party lines. Gen Z voters support aggressive climate action and expansive social programs, but they also express deep skepticism about institutional competence and a striking openness to economic policies — tariffs, industrial policy, even price controls — that the Democratic establishment considers heretical.

This ideological instability is not confusion; it is a rational response to a system that has not delivered for them. Homeownership rates among adults under 30 have fallen to their lowest level since the Great Depression. Real wages for workers aged 18 to 29 have stagnated for a decade while housing costs have risen 47 percent. The social contract that previous generations took for granted — work hard, get an education, buy a house, build a life — has been effectively broken for anyone who did not inherit wealth or happen to enter the right sector at the right time.

The political implications are still unfolding, but they are significant. Neither party has figured out how to speak to Gen Z's specific combination of economic anxiety and institutional distrust. Republicans offer tax cuts and deregulation — policies that address the concerns of business owners and investors, not 25-year-olds who cannot afford rent. Democrats offer student loan forgiveness and climate commitments — policies that address Gen Z's stated priorities but do nothing to fix the structural conditions that make those priorities necessary in the first place.

What Gen Z seems to want is something that neither party is currently structured to deliver: a political economy that provides stability without requiring the kind of lifetime commitment to a single career or employer that their grandparents' social contract demanded. They want the security of affordable housing, healthcare, and a reasonable work-life balance, but they want these things delivered through systems that are flexible enough to accommodate the gig work, career switching, and geographic mobility that define their working lives.

Whether any political party can build a coalition around this agenda is an open question. But the 2026 midterms made one thing clear: Gen Z is not waiting for either party to figure it out. They are voting, and they are voting in ways that make the old playbook obsolete.

## 文章大意

2026年中期选举成为Z世代的政治反叛。他们的经济体验是不稳定而非衰退，导致政治行为跨越传统左右轴线——既支持激进气候行动和扩大社会项目，又对制度能力深表怀疑，对关税和价格管制持开放态度。这种意识形态不稳定是对系统失效的理性回应：住房自有率跌至大萧条以来最低，实际工资停滞而住房成本上涨47%。两党都未能回应Z世代经济焦虑与制度不信任的组合，他们想要一个提供稳定性但不需要终身职业承诺的政治经济体系。
